# Cédula com setas — spec (draft, LucentSetas/1)

A ballot for **union elections**, the same in three channels: **by mail**, **in person** and **online**. The candidates are printed in a fixed public order; a strip of **arrows**, different on every ballot, leads each candidate to one of the boxes. The voter follows the arrows, marks boxes and destroys the part with the arrows. Without the arrows, the marked boxes say nothing. At the count, the ballots are shuffled, the arrows of the marked boxes are revealed, and **anyone can recount by following the arrows**.

Design priorities, in order: **correctness** that every voter and observer can check, then ballot secrecy. The arrows replace adding encrypted votes, because a shuffle followed by an open count is easier to understand.

Scope: elections with up to **k choices among n candidates** (for example 4 of 12 for a *conselho fiscal*, or 1 of 3 *chapas*), **200 to 500 voters** per polling place. Where this document says MUST, the behavior affects verification or secrecy.

## 1. Roles

| Role | Does |
|---|---|
| **Organizer** (the union) | Holds the voter list; runs the polling places. |
| **Committee** (*comissão eleitoral*), m members | Holds the committee's key share, protected by passwords, t of m needed (e.g. 3 of 5; section 2). Runs the second shuffle. |
| **LucentCrypto** | Prepares the software, the ballots and the scanning box; holds its own key share; runs the first shuffle. |
| **Print shop** | Prints the ballots from the ballot file. |
| **Poll workers** (*mesários*) | Check voters against the list, hand out ballots, guard the box. |
| **Observers** (the *chapas*, anyone) | Watch the ceremonies; verify the bulletin board. |
| **Bulletin board** | A public page where every item in section 7 is published and kept. |

## 2. Keys

ElGamal on ristretto255 (RFC 9496), the same group and library as `votacao/` and `cruzamento/`. `B` is the base point, ℓ its order.

- **Two shares.** LucentCrypto draws `s_L`, the committee draws `s_C`, each in [1, ℓ), each on its own device. Each publishes `P_L = s_L·B`, `P_C = s_C·B` with a Schnorr proof of knowledge. The election key is `P = P_L + P_C`; its secret `s_L + s_C` never exists in one place.
- **The committee's share** MUST be split t-of-m with Shamir's scheme over the scalar field, and `s_C` erased. Each member's piece is encrypted with a key derived (Argon2id) from a **passphrase generated by the software**: 6 words from a Portuguese list of at least 2048 words (at least 66 bits). Passphrases MUST NOT be chosen by people: the encrypted pieces could otherwise be attacked offline by whoever holds them.
- Each member receives a sealed envelope with the passphrase written out and the encrypted piece as a QR code. The pieces stay with the members; LucentCrypto keeps a copy only if the committee asks for it, and the contract says so.
- A committee of one (for example only the president) is the same structure with t = m = 1.
- The contract and the minutes MUST say: *the key has two parts, one with LucentCrypto and one with the committee, which t of its m members can unlock; no party alone can decrypt.*

## 3. The ballot

**Election parameters**, published before anything else: the candidates `1..n` in their printed order, the maximum number of choices `k`, the channels and their deadlines, the threshold t of m.

**One ballot** has:

- a random **permutation** π of `1..n`: candidate `i` is joined by an arrow to box `π(i)`;
- a **code**: 8 characters from an alphabet without look-alike characters (e.g. `K7F2-9QXM`), chosen at random, unique;
- the **encrypted arrows**: for each box `j`, the ElGamal encryption under `P` of the candidate behind it, `c_j = Enc(P, π⁻¹(j)·B)`. Candidates are small numbers, so a decryption is read by lookup.

The code is printed; the encrypted arrows are **not**: they go into the **ballot manifest**, the list of `(code, c_1 … c_n)`, which MUST be published (or its hash, with the full list before the count) before the first ballot is handed out. A ballot with a code not in the manifest is invalid.

**Layout** (A4, portrait; see the mockup):

- **Left part, to destroy:** the names in the fixed order; the arrow strip. Each arrow runs horizontally from the middle of a name row, turns up or down in a vertical column, and runs horizontally into its box. Arrows whose vertical stretches do not overlap share a column. The boxes are offset half a row down from the names, so an arrow leaving a row never runs on the same line as one arriving beside it.
- **A perforation** from the top edge to the bottom edge, about three quarters of the page width from the left.
- **Right part, to send:** the identification block (name, member number, signature) at the top, **separated from the rest by a horizontal perforation**; the n numbered boxes; the QR code with the code and the code in text at the bottom.

The code MUST NOT appear in the identification block, and nothing on the right part may vary with the candidates.

**Who sees the arrows.** The software that generates the ballot file and the print shop see the arrows of every code. They MUST NOT learn which ballot goes to which voter: ballots are handed out at random (section 4). The generating software erases π after writing the print file; that is a matter of trust in the software, which is open source.

## 4. Channels

The online and mail channels MUST close before in-person voting starts. The list of who voted by mail or online is then handed to the polling places, so nobody votes twice.

### 4.1 By mail

- The mailing step puts ballots into envelopes **at random** and MUST NOT record which code goes to which address.
- The voter marks, separates the parts, destroys the left part, fills in and signs the identification block, and posts the right part in one envelope.
- At reception: the identification is checked against the voter list and the voter is marked as voted. The identification block is then **torn off along its perforation**, and only the vote part goes on. A second ballot from the same voter is set aside, unopened, as a duplicate.
- The vote parts are scanned (code and marks) and kept for a recount.

### 4.2 In person

- The poll worker checks the voter against the list; the voter **picks a ballot at random** from the pile. On request, a voter may take two and hand one back for audit (section 5).
- In the booth the voter marks, separates the parts and destroys the left part (a small shredder or bin is in the booth). The identification block is not used in person; the list at the desk is the record.
- **The scanning box:** a Raspberry Pi with a camera at the slot of a sealed box. It reads the code and the marks and MUST return the part to the voter if the code is not in the manifest, is already used, or the part has more than k marks. Otherwise it shows *Cédula K7F2-9QX recebida* and lets the paper drop into the box. It never sees the arrows, so not even a compromised box can reveal a vote.
- At closing the box prints and signs the list of codes received; the images and the list are exported. The paper stays sealed for a recount.
- **Accessibility:** for a voter who cannot read the arrows, a Pi with a screen and headphones generates a ballot as the browser does (4.3). In that case the secrecy depends on that device's software.

### 4.3 Online

- The voter signs in. The **browser** generates π and the code, encrypts the arrows under `P` and sends `(code, c_1 … c_n)` to the board **before** showing the ballot. The server MUST NOT generate π.
- The ballot is shown with the same layout. The voter may **audit** it: the browser reveals the randomness of each `c_j`, anyone can check it against the arrows shown, and the ballot is discarded for a new one.
- The voter marks the boxes and submits `(code, marks)`. The browser then forgets π; the voter may keep a screenshot of the right part as a receipt.
- The server knows which voter submitted which code, as the mail reception does. Neither knows the arrows.
- The software is the same open-source code as the Pi's, served with a published hash.

## 5. Audits

- **Blank-ballot audit.** After printing, a random sample of the blank ballots is drawn, with a seed that the *chapas* contribute to in public. The sample's encrypted arrows are decrypted jointly (with proofs, section 6) and compared with the print; the audited codes are voided. If a fraction f of the ballots is wrong, a sample of s catches it with probability 1 − (1 − f)^s: s = 60 catches f = 5 % with 95 %.
- **Voter-initiated audit.** In person, the second ballot a voter hands back goes into an audit envelope and is decrypted at the count ceremony. Online, the audit is immediate (4.3).
- **Each voter** finds their code in the published table and compares the marks with their photo or screenshot.
- **Counts.** Per channel, the number of vote parts MUST equal the number of voters marked as voted; blank ballots handed out, returned, audited and unused MUST add up. All counts are published.
- **Recount.** The paper of the in-person box and of the mail is kept; anyone may compare it with the scanned table.

## 6. The count

1. **Rows.** For each cast ballot: the marked boxes `j₁ … j_r` (r ≤ k) give the row `(c_{j₁}, …, c_{j_r})`, padded to k entries with encryptions of 0 (*no choice*). The unmarked boxes are not used. Rows carry no code and no channel.
2. **Shuffle, twice.** LucentCrypto, then the committee, each re-encrypt every entry and permute the rows, with a **cut-and-choose proof** (Sako–Kilian): λ = 40 intermediate shuffles, challenge bits from a hash of the transcript, each opening either the input side or the output side. Nothing can be added, removed or changed without detection, except with probability 2⁻⁴⁰. One honest shuffle is enough for nobody to know which row came from which ballot.
3. **Reveal.** LucentCrypto and t committee members decrypt each entry of the shuffled rows: each side contributes its partial decryption with a Chaum–Pedersen proof of equality of discrete logarithms. The committee's share is reassembled from t passphrases on the ceremony device, used, and erased.
4. **Table.** Each row now reads as up to k candidate numbers. The table and the totals per candidate are published. Anyone can recount from the table, and check every proof, with any program.

Only the candidates behind the **marked** boxes are ever revealed; the rest of each permutation stays encrypted.

## 7. Bulletin board

In order: the election parameters; `P_L`, `P_C` with their proofs; the manifest hash, then the manifest; the seeds and results of the blank-ballot audit; the online `(code, encrypted arrows)` as they are created; at closing, the cast table `(code, marks, channel)` and the counts; the rows; each shuffle with its proof; each decryption with its proof; the result table and the totals.

## 8. Security

**Correctness**, without trusting anyone:

| Could go wrong | Caught by |
|---|---|
| Printed arrows that differ from the encrypted ones | Blank-ballot and voter audits |
| A vote part lost or changed after casting | Each voter's photo against the cast table; paper recount |
| A row added, dropped or changed in a shuffle | The shuffle proofs |
| A wrong decryption | The Chaum–Pedersen proofs |
| Ballots stuffed by an insider | Each cast ballot needs a listed voter; counts per channel must match |
| Voting twice across channels | Online and mail close first; one list |

**Secrecy.** Linking a voter to a vote needs the code of their ballot **and** its arrows before the shuffle:

- the mail reception and the online server know voter ↔ code, but not the arrows;
- the generating software and the print shop know code ↔ arrows, but not who received which ballot;
- decrypting arrows before the shuffle needs LucentCrypto **and** t committee members together;
- a compromised browser, or the accessibility Pi, sees the arrows while the voter votes. The scanning box never does.

**Coercion.** At home (mail, online) nothing prevents someone from watching the voter, as with any remote vote. Because each row is revealed, a coercer may demand an unusual combination and look for it in the table (the "Italian attack"): with 4 of 12 there are 794 combinations for a few hundred voters. Accepted, and stated to the organizer.

**Out of scope:** a voter who keeps or photographs the left part; denial of service; the voter list itself.

## 9. Open points

- The cut-and-choose shuffle with λ = 40 on 500 rows of 4 entries: measure the time in a browser, or use a Bayer–Groth proof.
- Reading marks with a camera: tolerance for faint, partial or crossed-out marks; what counts as a mark.
- The Portuguese word list for passphrases.
- Seeds for audits: how the *chapas* contribute (dice, numbers read out in public).
- Whether to publish the scanned images of the vote parts, or only the table.
