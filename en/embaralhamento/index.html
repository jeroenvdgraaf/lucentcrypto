Verifiable shuffle

Português

Whoever publishes a list without names, such as a company’s salaries or the bids in a public tender, also seals an envelope with each participant’s value. Then they prove that the envelopes contain exactly the published list, in another order, without revealing who has which value.

1The data and one round of the proof

STEP 1 · PROVERShuffle and seal

Picks a secret order and puts each value of the list in a new box, sealed with fresh randomness: D = gvalue·hr′.

STEP 2 · VERIFIERToss a coin

0: show which box belongs to whom, without opening any. 1: open the boxes and show how they match the public list.

STEP 3 · VERIFIERCheck the answer

Waiting for the coin.

    2Repeat the round

    One round catches a lie with probability ½. After k independent rounds, a lie gets through with probability 2−k. Here the coin is truly random in every round.

    3Proof (Fiat–Shamir)

    With nobody to toss the coin: the prover first seals the boxes for all 80 rounds and computes the SHA-256 of everything public (the group, the participants, the list, the envelopes C and all the boxes D). Bit t of that hash is the coin of round t. Anyone can recompute the hash and check the 80 answers afterwards.

    To cheat, one would have to redo the boxes until the hash lands on the prepared coins: about 280 attempts.

    The proof contains the names and the public list of values, which are already public. It never says who has which value.

    In each round, a liar can only prepare for one of the two questions. If the coin lands on the other, the lie shows.

    What this proof guarantees, and what it does not

    It proves that the values sealed in the envelopes are exactly the public list, in another order. It does not reveal who has which value, and it does not say whether the values themselves are true: that depends on whoever publishes them. We use the “cut and choose” technique because it can be followed step by step; real systems usually use compact proofs, equivalent but far more technical.
